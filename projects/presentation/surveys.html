Survey Methods in Marketing

And Why They Don't Always Work

The Big Problem

Customers don’t know what they want — or can’t articulate it.

  • They say one thing and do another
  • They avoid trade-offs
  • They anchor on numbers you give them
  • They want to look rational or frugal
“If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses.”

Why Traditional Surveys Fail

Direct questions → unreliable answers

Hypothetical Bias

No real consequences → inflated answers

  • “Would you buy this?” → Yes (but they won’t)
  • “How much would you pay?” → Unrealistic numbers

Social Desirability Bias

People want to look good

  • Overstate eco-friendly choices
  • Understate luxury spending

Scale-Use Bias

One person’s 8/10 is another’s 5/10

  • Cultural differences
  • Personal rating habits

Anchoring

The first number shapes all responses

  • “Would you pay $199?” → anchors future answers

No Trade-Offs

Asking “How important is X?” → everything becomes “very important”

Real-World Survey Failures

  • “Rate these features” → everything is a 7–10
  • “How much would you pay?” → anchored, inflated
  • “Would you buy this?” → 70% say yes, 5% actually buy

So How Do Marketers Figure Out What People Really Want?

Modern Marketing Research

Methods designed to overcome human bias

MaxDiff

Problem it solves: Everything is “important”

How it works: Show 4–5 items → pick MOST and LEAST important

Why it works:

  • Forces prioritization
  • Removes scale bias
  • Produces clear rankings

Conjoint Analysis

Problem it solves: People can’t tell you their true preferences

How it works: Show product bundles → choose one

Why it works:

  • Simulates real shopping
  • Forces trade-offs
  • Reveals willingness to pay
  • Predicts market share for new concepts

Pricing Methods

  • Van Westendorp: acceptable price range
  • Gabor-Granger: sequential “Would you buy at $X?”
  • Monadic price tests: different groups see different prices

Why These Methods Work

  • They mimic real decisions
  • They force trade-offs
  • They reduce bias
  • They avoid scale problems
  • They extract preferences indirectly

Real-World Examples

Coca-Cola

Uses conjoint to design new flavors and packaging

Bose

Uses conjoint to price headphones and feature bundles

Airlines

Use conjoint to price seat bundles (legroom, bags, boarding)

Tech Companies

Use MaxDiff to prioritize features in product roadmaps

The Big Takeaway

Direct questions → unreliable answers

Choice-based methods → real preferences

Thank You

Good marketers don’t ask customers what they want — they design surveys that reveal it.
