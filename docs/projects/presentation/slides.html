The Psychology of Marketing Surveys

How Conjoint and MaxDiff use language, trade-offs, and choice design

Author

Sophia Salas

Published

May 1, 2026

Why Survey Language Matters

Marketing surveys are not neutral. The words we use shape how customers think, what identity they perform, and which trade-offs they notice.

Traditional survey wording

  • “Would you buy this?”
  • “How important is this feature?”
  • “What would you pay?”
  • “Rate this 1–10.”

Choice-based survey wording

  • “Which option would you choose?”
  • “Which is most and least important?”
  • “Would you buy at this price?”
  • “Which bundle feels worth it?”

Main idea: Better marketing surveys do not just ask for preferences. They design situations where preferences are revealed through choices.

Framing

Language changes what people notice.

Identity

Answers reflect how people want to appear.

Trade-offs

Better questions make sacrifice unavoidable.

Sources: Bettman, Luce & Payne; Tversky & Kahneman;

Conjoint: Building a Realistic Shopping Task

Conjoint changes the question from “What features do you like?” to “Which product bundle would you choose?”

How conjoint is constructed

  • Choose product attributes
  • Define levels for each attribute
  • Build choice tasks from different combinations
  • Randomize and balance screens
  • Include a none option when realistic
  • Estimate part-worth utilities for each level

Example: Subscription bundle

Attribute Option A Option B
Price $49/mo $59/mo
Delivery Fast Standard
Support Basic Premium
Packaging Standard Eco-friendly

Which option would you choose? Or neither?

Why “none” matters: without it, respondents may be forced to choose a product they would never actually buy.

Sources: Green & Srinivasan.

Why Conjoint Works: Trade-Offs Over Wish Lists

Compared with direct surveys

Direct questions isolate features:

  • “Do you like fast delivery?”
  • “Is sustainability important?”
  • “Would you pay more?”
  • “What is your ideal product?”

Problem: customers can want everything in theory.

Conjoint forces realistic compromise

Choice tasks combine positives and negatives:

  • Fast shipping, but higher price
  • Eco packaging, but slower delivery
  • Premium support, but less convenience
  • Better brand, but fewer features

Result: marketers estimate what people actually give up for each feature.

Product

Which features create value?

Price

What are people willing to pay?

Segmentation

Which groups value different bundles?

Choice tasks → part-worth utilities → willingness to pay → product and price decisions

Sources: Green & Srinivasan

The Psychology Behind Conjoint

Conjoint works because it transforms vague stated preferences into structured, multi-attribute choice behavior.

Psychological theories

Multi-Attribute Utility Theory

Products are bundles of attributes. Total value comes from the perceived value of each attribute level.

Theory of Conjoint Measurement

Qualitative judgments like “I prefer A to B” can be translated into numerical preference scales.

Random Utility Theory

Choice has a systematic part we can model and a random part we cannot fully observe.

How conjoint uses them

  • Varies attributes and levels across tasks
  • Estimates part-worth utilities from choices
  • Treats price as one attribute among many
  • Models imperfect but predictable choice behavior
  • Reduces vague self-reporting by forcing bundle-level decisions

The survey does not ask people to explain their utility function. It infers it from choices.

Bias mitigation: social desirability is weaker when sustainability, price, brand, and convenience are bundled together. Respondents must trade off the full package.

Sources: Bettman, Luce & Payne; Green & Srinivasan

MaxDiff: Building a Prioritization Task

MaxDiff changes the question from “How important is each item?” to “Which is most and least important?”

How MaxDiff is constructed

  • Start with a long list of features, claims, or benefits
  • Show small sets of 3–6 items
  • Ask for the most and least preferred
  • Repeat across multiple screens
  • Balance item exposure and pairings
  • Estimate a preference score for every item

Example: Feature priorities

MOST: Lower price

Better battery life

Better camera

LEAST: Brand reputation

Key difference from rating scales: respondents cannot mark every feature as “very important.” They must prioritize.

Sources: Qualtrics MaxDiff overview.

The Psychology Behind MaxDiff

Psychological theories

Thurstonian Scaling

When people compare items, each item creates an underlying psychological value that can be recovered from repeated judgments.

Elimination by Aspects

People simplify complex choices by focusing on attributes that matter and eliminating weaker options.

Random Utility Theory

People choose the option or pair with the highest perceived utility difference, plus some noise.

How MaxDiff uses them

  • Asks for extremes because best/worst is easier than exact ranking
  • Breaks long lists into small sets to reduce cognitive load
  • Forces trade-offs to prevent “everything is important”
  • Removes scale-use bias by avoiding 1–10 ratings
  • Produces a relative hierarchy of preferences

MaxDiff works because people are better at comparative judgment than absolute scoring.

Core principle: MaxDiff turns psychological comparison into marketing data.

Sources: Marley & Louviere / best-worst choice models; Tversky, Elimination by Aspects

Conclusion: Better Surveys Create Better Segments

Conjoint and MaxDiff work because they turn vague self-reporting into structured choice behavior — and structured choices reveal how different customers actually value things.

Weak survey design gives…

  • Inflated purchase intent
  • Vague ratings where everything scores high
  • “Everything is important”
  • Averages that hide meaningful differences

Choice-based survey design gives…

  • Realistic trade-offs
  • Part-worth utilities
  • Willingness to pay estimates
  • Preference scores
  • Preference-based segments

Price-sensitive

Responds to value language and low-price framing.

Quality-seeking

Responds to performance language and feature detail.

Identity-driven

Responds to lifestyle language and brand signals.

Good marketing surveys do not just ask better questions.

They use psychology to reveal what different customers value, choose, and respond to.
