Speaker Script — The Psychology of Marketing Surveys

Title Slide

Hi everyone. Today I’m talking about the psychology of marketing surveys — specifically how conjoint analysis and MaxDiff use language, trade-offs, and choice design to reveal what customers actually value.

A survey question is not just collecting an answer. It is creating a psychological situation. The moment a respondent reads a question, they are interpreting it, thinking about how they want to appear, and constructing a response on the spot. That process is deeply influenced by how the question is worded, what alternatives are shown, and what trade-offs are made unavoidable.

What I want to emphasize today is the importanceof treating surveys as, not, neutral question-and-answer tools, but designed decision environments. The design of that environment determines whether you get honest, behaviorally realistic data — or inflated, socially desirable noise.

Conjoint and MaxDiff are two techniques that take this seriously. They are not just better question formats. They are methods built on specific psychological theories about how people make decisions. That is what I want to walk through today.

Slide 1: Why Survey Language Matters

Let me start with why the wording of a survey question matters so much.

When we ask someone “Would you buy this product?”, we are making the decision artificially easy. There is no money changing hands. Marketing surveys are not neutral and the words and phrasing used shapes the way customers think and force tradeoffs.

Traditional Surveys ask questions like: “would you buy this, how important is this, what would you pay, rate this” The issue here: no choices are bieng forces? Would you buy this? sure? how important is this? everything is important? what would you pay? we would all love to not pay. rate this 1-10? what is 1 and what is 10?

Choice-based wording changes all of this. When we ask “Which of these two options would you choose?” or “Which feature is most important and which is least important?”, we are forcing the respondent to compare alternatives and commit to a trade-off. We have moved from asking people to describe themselves — which invites identity performance — to asking them to make a decision — which reveals actual priorities.

First Principle or foundational principle in economics and psychology is that humans have the ability to compare two options and state a preference. This kind of foundational principle does not exist for importance ratings on scales of 1-10.

  • Rating Scale Relativity: When a person rates something a “7” and another thing an “8”, traditional research assumes the distance between those numbers is identical for everyone. In reality, one person’s “8” might mean the exact same thing as another person’s “6
  • Cultural and personal bias: Some people are generous raters (mostly 9s and 10s), while others are strict (rarely giving anything above a 7).

As we have seen:

Framing: language changes how people think

Identity: If we ask “How important is sustainability to you?”, many people will say very important — not necessarily because they will actually pay more for a sustainable product, but because sustainability is part of the identity they want to project. They are answering as the kind of person they want to be, not necessarily as the kind of person who actually makes purchase decisions.

Tradeoffs: better questions force sacrifice

These elements are all at the core of conjoint and MaxDiff.

Slide 2: Conjoint — Building a Realistic Shopping Task

Conjoint analysis is built around one central idea: force respondents to make trade-offs by presenting realistic product bundles, the same way they would choose in an actual market.

The question changes from “What features do you like?” — which allows wish-list thinking — to “Which product bundle would you choose?” — which requires sacrifice.

Here is how it works in practice. First, you choose the product attributes you want to study. These are the dimensions that define the product (let’s say this is a subscrption bundle service for something like i dont know the factor meal service): price, customer support, packaging type, and so on. Then, for each attribute, you define levels — the specific values that attribute can take. Price might be $49 per month or $59 per month. Obviously here one might appear better than another option but this is just for the example purpose. Remember we randomly assign different versions of these screens to different respondents, ensuring that all features and combinations are tested equitably.

You then combine these levels into product profiles. Each profile is a realistic bundle — one level per attribute. The respondent has to decide which combination of trade-offs works best for them.

This is psychologically important for a specific reason: it mirrors the structure of real purchase decisions. When someone buys a product or service, they are almost never choosing between a perfect option and a bad one. They are choosing between imperfect bundles where different features pull in different directions. Conjoint recreates that structure deliberately.

There is also a specific design choice worth explaining: the “none” option. In many conjoint tasks, respondents are given the choice to select neither option — to say they would not buy either bundle at all. This matters because without it, you can create artificial demand. If you force someone to choose between Option A and Option B, you will get a choice — but that choice might not mean anything if in reality the respondent would have walked away from both. Including “none” gives respondents the same exit that the real market gives them. It makes the task more honest.

From the choices respondents make across many tasks, we can statistically estimate how much each attribute level contributes to the overall attractiveness of a product. These are called part-worth utilities. They tell us not just whether people prefer , but exactly how much they prefer it relative to other things they care about — and how much price it would take to make them give it up.

## Slide 3: Why Conjoint Works
Traditional survey questions often isolate features and ask about them one at a time. “Do you like fast delivery?” Yes. “Is sustainability important?” Yes. “Would you pay more for a premium product?” Maybe. The problem is that people can say yes to all of these at once because there is no real sacrifice.
Conjoint works because it replaces wish-list thinking with trade-off thinking. Instead of asking people to rate isolated features, it asks them to choose between imperfect bundles. That is much closer to actual shopping, where better delivery might come with a higher price, or eco-friendly packaging might come with slower shipping.
A good example of why this matters is the attitude-behavior gap discussed by Auger and Devinney in 2007. Consumers often say ethical or sustainable attributes are extremely important, but when they are placed in constrained trade-off situations, price and product performance still matter a lot. So the stated preference is “I care most about sustainability,” but the revealed preference may be “I care about sustainability only if the price and performance are still acceptable.”
This is why conjoint is so valuable in marketing. It tells us not just whether people like a feature, but what they are willing to give up to get that feature. That makes it useful for product design, pricing, willingness to pay, and segmentation.

Slide 4: The Psychology Behind Conjoint

Slide 4: The Psychology Behind Conjoint

The main psychological assumption behind conjoint is a compensatory utility model. In simple terms, that means people evaluate a product by adding up the value of its attributes. A product might have a low score on one attribute, like price, but a high score on another attribute, like quality, and those can compensate for each other.

The model is often written like this: utility equals attribute one times its weight, plus attribute two times its weight, plus some random error. In other words, (U_j = x_{1j}1 + x{2j}_2 + _j). That is the basic logic behind part-worth utilities.

This connects to Multi-Attribute Utility Theory. That theory says products are bundles of attributes, and the total value of a product comes from the perceived value of its parts: price, brand, quality, delivery, design, convenience, and so on. Conjoint uses this directly by breaking products into attributes and levels, then inferring the value of each level from choices.

It also connects to Random Utility Theory. Random Utility Theory says that choice has a systematic part we can model, like price and features, and a random part we cannot fully observe, like mood, attention, context, or personal taste. That is why conjoint models predict probabilities rather than perfect individual decisions.

But this is also where we need to be careful. The compensatory model is an assumption. In real life, some decisions are non-compensatory. For example, if a person refuses to buy a phone because it lacks a headphone jack, no amount of storage or discount may compensate for that missing feature. Or if someone strongly dislikes a brand, a low price might still not be enough.

That is different from the compensatory logic in conjoint. It is closer to theories like Elimination by Aspects, where people screen out options that fail on an important attribute before they even consider the rest of the bundle.

Slide 5: MaxDiff — Building a Prioritization Task

MaxDiff, or Maximum Difference Scaling, uses the same fundamental principle as conjoint: people are better at comparing options than assigning absolute scores.

The problem MaxDiff solves is rating-scale bias. If we ask people to rate ten features from one to ten, many respondents will rate almost everything as important. Some people are generous raters and give mostly nines and tens. Other people are strict raters and rarely give anything above a seven. That makes ratings hard to compare across people.

MaxDiff changes the task. Instead of asking, “How important is each item?” it asks, “Which item is most important and which is least important?” Respondents see a small set of items, usually three to six, and choose the extremes.

This is psychologically easier because humans are generally good at identifying extremes. It is much easier to say “this matters most” and “this matters least” than to precisely rank fifteen items or decide whether something is a seven or an eight.

MaxDiff is also closely related to conjoint. You can think of it as a simpler conjoint-style model where each item has its own utility: (U_j = _j + _j). In conjoint, utility comes from product attributes. In MaxDiff, utility is attached to the item itself. Both methods collect choice data that can be analyzed with MNL-style logic.

Slide 6: The Psychology Behind MaxDiff

The psychology behind MaxDiff starts with comparative judgment. A fundamental principle in economics and psychology is that people can usually look at two options and say which one they prefer. There is no equally clean principle for what a “7 out of 10 importance rating” means.

Thurstonian Scaling, or the Law of Comparative Judgment, says that when people compare items, each item creates an underlying psychological value. That value is noisy, but if we observe enough comparisons, we can recover the underlying preference structure. MaxDiff uses repeated best-worst choices to reconstruct that preference scale without asking respondents to assign numbers.

Random Utility Theory also applies. In a MaxDiff task, the respondent chooses the item with the highest perceived utility as “most” and the item with the lowest perceived utility as “least.” There is still noise, which is why we need repeated tasks, but the pattern reveals systematic preference.

Elimination by Aspects is a different psychological theory that helps explain how people simplify complex choices. It says people may focus on one important aspect and eliminate options that fail on that aspect. This is different from the compensatory conjoint model, where a bad attribute can be offset by a good one. Elimination by Aspects is non-compensatory.

So MaxDiff is not exactly the same thing as Elimination by Aspects, but the theory helps explain why small best-worst sets feel natural. They let people simplify the decision and focus on the clearest differences.

The key takeaway is that MaxDiff works because it respects how people actually compare. It avoids absolute scoring, reduces scale-use bias, lowers cognitive load, and forces a true hierarchy of priorities.

Conclusion: Better Surveys Create Better Segments

The big takeaway I want to leave you with is this: conjoint and MaxDiff work because they turn vague self-reporting into structured choice behavior — and structured choice behavior reveals how different customers actually differ from one another.

When survey design is weak, you get data that obscures real differences. You get inflated purchase intent because people say yes without a trade-off. You get vague ratings where everything scores important. You get averages that blend together price-sensitive shoppers and quality-seeking shoppers as if they were the same person. The data describes the average respondent, who might not actually exist in your market.

Choice-based survey design gives you something fundamentally more useful. Part-worth utilities tell you the exact value customers place on each attribute level. Willingness-to-pay estimates tell you how much different features are worth in dollar terms. Preference scores from MaxDiff tell you what customers care about enough to prioritize. And from all of that, you can build segments that are grounded in how people actually make decisions — not in how they describe themselves on a scale.

Those segments are immediately actionable. A price-sensitive segment responds to value framing and low-price signals. A quality-seeking segment responds to performance language and detailed feature comparisons. An identity-driven segment responds to lifestyle language, brand story, and social signals. Knowing which segment you are talking to means you can build different products, set different prices, and design different messages for each.

The larger point is that good marketing research is not just about asking better questions. It is about understanding the psychology of how people respond to questions — and designing surveys that work with that psychology rather than ignoring it. Conjoint and MaxDiff do that by treating the survey itself as a decision environment: one that is carefully controlled, behaviorally grounded, and designed to reveal real preferences rather than reported ones.

This is also why these methods are useful for segmentation: they do not just tell us what the average customer says. They reveal which customers make different trade-offs, which is what marketers actually need for targeting.
